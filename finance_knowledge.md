## Market Basics

- Stocks represent partial ownership of a company; bonds are loans to a company or government.
- An ETF (exchange-traded fund) bundles many assets into one tradeable fund; index funds track an index (e.g. S&P 500).
- Market capitalization = share price × shares outstanding. Large-cap > $10B, mid-cap $2–10B, small-cap < $2B.
- A bull market is a sustained rise; a bear market is a sustained decline (commonly −20% from recent highs).
- Liquidity is how easily an asset can be bought/sold without moving its price.
- Diversification spreads risk across assets/sectors so no single failure dominates.
- Dollar-cost averaging (DCA): investing a fixed amount on a schedule, which smooths purchase prices over time.
- Compounding: returns earn returns; time in the market generally matters more than timing the market.
- Inflation erodes purchasing power; historically ~2–3% per year on average.
- The S&P 500's long-run average annual return is roughly 7–10% before inflation (varies by period).
- Index investing often beats most active managers over long horizons after fees.
- US stock markets open 9:30 AM and close 4:00 PM Eastern Time, Monday–Friday (holidays excluded).

## Valuation Ratios & Fundamentals

- P/E ratio = price ÷ earnings per share. High P/E may mean growth expected or overvaluation; compare to industry and history.
- P/B ratio = price ÷ book value per share. Low P/B can indicate undervaluation (or troubled assets).
- EPS (earnings per share) = net income ÷ shares outstanding. Growing EPS is a common quality signal.
- ROE (return on equity) = net income ÷ shareholders' equity. Higher and stable ROE is generally good.
- Debt-to-equity = total liabilities ÷ equity. High debt increases risk, especially when rates rise.
- Dividend yield = annual dividend ÷ price. High yield can signal value or distress; check payout ratio.
- Payout ratio = dividends ÷ earnings. Above ~100% is usually unsustainable.
- Revenue growth, gross margin, operating margin, and free cash flow are core health signals.
- Free cash flow = operating cash flow − capital expenditures; it funds dividends, buybacks, and growth.
- Compare a company's ratios to its own history and its sector — a single number in isolation means little.

## Technical Indicators & Charting

- Moving average (MA): average price over N periods; the 50-day and 200-day MAs are widely watched.
- Golden cross: 50-day MA crossing above the 200-day MA — a bullish signal. Death cross is the opposite.
- RSI (relative strength index, 0–100): above 70 is often "overbought," below 30 "oversold"; not a standalone signal.
- MACD: difference between 12-day and 26-day EMAs; crossovers and divergence are common signals.
- Support: a price level where buying historically appears; resistance: a level where selling appears.
- Breakouts above resistance (or below support) on volume are watched by traders.
- Moving averages smooth noise; indicators lag price and can give false signals in choppy markets.
- Volume confirms moves: a breakout on high volume is more credible than one on low volume.
- No indicator predicts the future; they describe what already happened.

## When to Consider Investing

- Only invest money you can afford to leave invested for years; emergency savings come first (3–6 months of expenses).
- Prefer a diversified, low-cost approach (index funds/ETFs) over picking single stocks as the core of a portfolio.
- Recurring, scheduled investing (DCA) removes emotion and catches both highs and lows.
- A company with growing revenue, stable margins, low manageable debt, positive free cash flow, and sensible valuation is a reasonable candidate for research.
- Check the valuation: is the P/E far above its historical range or the sector's? Extreme premiums need strong justification.
- Consider your own time horizon and risk tolerance before any purchase.
- Understand what you are buying: read the business model, financials, and risks — if it cannot be explained simply, keep researching.

## When to Consider Selling

- Sell when the original investment thesis is broken (business fundamentals deteriorated), not merely because the price fell.
- Rebalancing: if one holding grows far beyond its target weight, trimming restores your intended risk level.
- Taking profits can be rational after a large run-up when valuation becomes extreme relative to fundamentals.
- Tax-loss harvesting: selling a losing position to offset gains can be tax-efficient (consult a tax professional).
- Avoid selling out of panic during routine volatility; bear markets are often when long-term investors buy.
- Trailing-stop or target-price rules can remove emotion — decide the rule before buying, not during a drop.
- Frequent trading increases fees, taxes, and the odds of buying high / selling low.

## Risk Management

- Position sizing: no single stock should be so large that its loss devastates the portfolio; many advisors cap single positions at 5–10%.
- Diversify across sectors, geographies, and asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash, real estate).
- Correlation matters: "diversification" across companies in the same sector is weaker than it looks.
- Volatility is not the same as risk of permanent loss; a diversified portfolio's dips are usually temporary.
- Leverage/margin amplifies losses and can force selling at the worst time — beginners should avoid it.
- A written plan (asset allocation, rebalancing schedule, sell rules) protects decisions from emotion.
- Fees compound: a 1% annual fee drags returns significantly over decades; favor low-cost funds.
- Past performance does not guarantee future results — always the fine print for a reason.

## Common Strategies & Concepts

- Buy and hold: long-term ownership of quality assets, relying on compounding and dividends.
- Value investing: buy assets below their intrinsic value (low P/E, low P/B, strong cash flow) and wait for the market to agree.
- Growth investing: buy companies with above-average expected earnings growth; expect higher volatility and valuation risk.
- Dividend investing: prioritize steady, growing dividends for income and stability.
- Index investing: own the whole market at low cost; historically the most reliable path for most investors.
- Diversification vs. concentration: concentration can outperform but carries far more single-stock risk.
- Rebalancing: periodically restore target weights (e.g. yearly), selling what rose and buying what fell.
- Risk tolerance questionnaire: aggressive (mostly stocks), balanced (mix), conservative (bonds/cash heavy).
- Asset allocation drives most of a portfolio's long-term returns — more than individual stock selection.
- Always have a cash buffer for opportunities and emergencies so you are never a forced seller.

## Behavioral Finance

- Fear and greed drive most poor decisions; markets are often most dangerous when everyone is optimistic.
- Loss aversion: losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good — plan around it.
- Herding: buying because "everyone else is" usually means buying after the move already happened.
- Confirmation bias: seeking news that agrees with a position you already hold leads to blind spots.
- Anchoring: fixating on the price you paid ("I'll wait until it's back to $X") is not an investment strategy.
- Sunk cost fallacy: what you paid is gone; only the current and expected future matter.
- FOMO (fear of missing out) leads to chasing tops; patience and a plan beat adrenaline.
- The best investors are often the most boring: a plan, diversification, and discipline over decades.
